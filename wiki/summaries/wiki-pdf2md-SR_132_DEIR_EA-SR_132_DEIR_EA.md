This document is a Draft Environmental Impact Report/Environmental Assessment (DEIR/EA) and Draft Final Remedial Action Plan (RAP) for the California Department of Transportation's (Caltrans) SR 132 West Freeway/Expressway Project in Modesto, Stanislaus County. The project, subject to both NEPA and CEQA guidelines, aims to improve regional and interregional circulation, relieve congestion, and enhance traffic operations by upgrading SR 132 (Maze Boulevard) and SR 99.

A significant component of the project involves the remediation of three Caltrans Modesto Soil Stockpiles, located within the project's right-of-way. These stockpiles, containing approximately 160,000 cubic yards of soil contaminated primarily with barium, strontium, and lead from the former Food Machinery and Chemical Corporation (FMC) site, are central to the remediation efforts. After evaluating various options (No Action, Institutional Controls, Removal), the recommended "Containment Alternative" will integrate the stockpiles into the highway construction. This method involves containing the contaminated soil behind retaining walls, bridge abutments, and beneath highway pavements, effectively remediating them within the project's framework. This phased remediation is estimated to cost $1.57 million, included in the total project cost of $210-214 million, with Phase 1 completion by 2020 and Phase 2 by 2028. This approach is deemed effective, technically feasible, and cost-effective, minimizing contaminant migration. If a "No-Build Alternative" is chosen for the highway, Caltrans would need a separate remedial action plan for the stockpiles.

The project addresses existing SR 132's high traffic volumes, speeds, numerous conflict points, and accident rates. Transportation Demand Management, Transportation System Management, Mass Transit, and the No-Build Alternative were all deemed insufficient to meet projected traffic needs and project objectives. The "Build" alternatives (Alternative 1 and Alternative 2) are expected to achieve these objectives by shifting traffic to a new alignment.

Caltrans owns and operates the stockpiles and is responsible for project design and construction. The California Department of Toxic Substances Control (DTSC) and the Central Valley Regional Water Quality Control Board (CVRWQCB) serve as CEQA responsible agencies, overseeing all remedial actions, including requiring approval of the Final RAP and a Remedial Design Implementation Plan. Ongoing monitoring of the stockpiles has, to date, shown no significant impacts from airborne dispersion or groundwater migration, and stormwater runoff monitoring will continue.

While the project is largely consistent with land use and transportation plans and is not expected to significantly affect coastal zones, forests, mineral resources, sensitive natural communities, special-status plants, wetlands, or wild and scenic rivers, it will result in several unavoidable environmental impacts. Both "Build" alternatives will convert 64.8 acres of prime and unique farmland to non-agricultural use, contributing to a "significant cumulative impact" on agricultural resources. "Significant cumulative impacts" are also projected for visual resources due to new highway structures and lighting, and for noise, affecting up to 276 receivers. Construction activities will lead to temporary increases in noise and equipment emissions, and air quality analysis projects NOx emissions exceeding 2 tons per year in Phase 2, requiring mitigation. The project area has low potential for landslides or slope instability.

Public and agency coordination, including regulatory concurrences from entities like the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency and U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, has been ongoing since 2010. The DEIR/EA was widely distributed to numerous governmental agencies, Native American tribes, and utility providers for comment. Public comments on the DEIR/EA are encouraged by March 3, 2017. Although the project will have "unavoidable significant environmental effects" regarding noise and visual/aesthetics (particularly a direct-connector flyover ramp near the Elm Tract neighborhood), and "significant cumulative impacts" on agricultural resources, visual resources, and noise, it is compliant with CEQA and NEPA requirements. The project is ultimately expected to reduce overall greenhouse gas emissions in the study area by alleviating congestion.
