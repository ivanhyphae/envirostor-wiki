# Nickel

## Overview

Nickel is a metal identified at the Caltrans Modesto Soil Stockpiles as a presumed component of petroleum coke, one of the raw materials used by the [[fmc-corporation]] facility during its chemical processing operations. Nickel was analyzed in soil samples from the stockpiles as part of the Title 22 metals suite, and its presence is evaluated in the context of the site's supplemental-site-investigation-report|Supplemental Site Investigation (SSI) and Human Health Risk Assessment (HHRA). Nickel is not identified as a primary chemical of potential concern (COPC) for the project site, though it is tracked as an analyte of interest due to its association with historical FMC operations.

## Site record

Nickel was included in the analytical testing program for soil samples collected during the 2012 Supplemental Site Investigation, as well as in earlier investigations conducted by Shaw Environmental in 2004, 2006, and 2007. The analytical method used was EPA Test Method 6010B (or 6020 for ICP-MS), and results were reported in milligrams per kilogram (mg/kg).

In the DTSC Human and Ecological Risk Office (HERO) memorandum dated February 14, 2013 (Appendix A of the revised SSI Report), nickel is listed in Table 2 — "Chemicals found in Caltrans Modesto Soil Stockpiles." The HERO memorandum notes that nickel, along with vanadium and carcinogenic PAHs, are presumed components of petroleum coke, a material identified in the FMC Corporation's raw materials and intermediates list (Table 1 of the HERO memorandum). The DTSC considers nickel to have toxic properties, though the memorandum emphasizes that barium has "substantially less toxicity than arsenic and petroleum coke."

The HERO evaluation of chemicals in the soil stockpiles, as presented in the February 14, 2013 memorandum, states that the maximum detected concentration of nickel — measured across multiple site investigation events from 2004, 2006, and 2012 — is less than its risk-based screening levels. The HERO made two conservative assumptions in its evaluation: first, that the maximum concentration of each chemical detected in the stockpile is the concentration to which human receptors would be exposed on a daily basis (rather than an average concentration over an area); and second, that these maximum concentrations are compared to the most conservative risk-based screening levels available, which are calculated for residential land use.

The HERO concluded that nickel, along with other metals listed in Table 2, was detected at concentrations below their respective risk-based screening levels and therefore does not represent a significant risk or hazard. The memorandum notes that while maximum concentrations of most metals listed in Table 2 exceed local background concentrations, none of these chemicals were detected at levels approaching their risk-based levels.

The 2012 SSI Fenceline Boring data (Table 2 of the revised SSI Report) includes nickel among the Title 22 metals analyzed in soil samples from the 35 fenceline borings (FB1–FB36) and the 28 perimeter borings (PB1–PB28). The report states that, with the exception of barium and lead, "the remaining Title 22 metals concentrations generally fall within the range of the site-specific naturally occurring background." Nickel is not specifically called out as exceeding any screening thresholds in the SSI's summary of findings.

## Regulatory status

Applicable screening levels for nickel referenced in the project documents include:

- **California Human Health Screening Levels (CHHSLs)** for residential and industrial land use, developed by the California Environmental Protection Agency (Cal/EPA) Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment (OEHHA). The CHHSLs are concentrations below which chemicals are considered to be below thresholds of concern for risks to human health (excess lifetime cancer risk of one in a million and a hazard quotient of 1.0 for noncancer effects).
- **U.S. EPA Regional Screening Levels (RSLs)** for residential properties, which are used as preliminary remediation goals for soil at sites being initially investigated.

The DTSC HERO memorandum (February 14, 2013) states that nickel concentrations in the soil stockpiles are below both the U.S. EPA residential RSL and the Cal/EPA residential CHHSL. The exact numeric values for nickel's screening levels are not provided in the source documents reviewed.

The overall regulatory conclusion, as documented in the HERO memorandum, is that the soil stockpiles do not pose a cancer risk or non-cancer hazard to persons in the vicinity of the stockpiles as long as the stockpiles remain in place and are properly managed. This conclusion applies to nickel as part of the broader chemical suite evaluated.

## Open items

The DTSC HERO memorandum includes a caveat that the evaluation presented is based on concentrations measured in surface soil. The memorandum states: "There are areas in the stockpiles with elevated concentrations of chemicals at depths greater than one foot below ground surface. Therefore, if there is substantial grading or reworking of the stockpiles or if the stockpiles are removed, these elevated concentrations at depth will have to be evaluated with respect to the potential for exposure by residents living adjacent or near the stockpiles during the period when the soil is being moved." This applies to nickel and all other chemicals of concern evaluated in the stockpiles.

Additionally, the SSI Report (Section 6.5) notes that Geocon is preparing an update to the Shaw 2007 HHRA, and that a Remedial Action Plan (RAP) is being developed to address the selected final remedy. Future evaluation of nickel may be part of these ongoing activities, particularly if subsurface disturbance occurs during construction of the State Route 132 West Freeway/Expressway Project.
