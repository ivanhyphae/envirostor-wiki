# Draft Final Remedial Action Plan

## Overview

The Draft Final Remedial Action Plan (RAP) is a key document for the Caltrans Modesto Soil Stockpiles Site, which is part of the SR 132 West Freeway/Expressway Project in Modesto, Stanislaus County, California. Prepared by Geocon Consultants, Inc. for Caltrans, the RAP, dated October 27, 2014, details site characterization findings, human health risk assessments, an evaluation of remedial alternatives, and the recommended remedy. It serves as a supplement to the project's Environmental Impact Report (EIR) under the California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA), with the Department of Toxic Substances Control (DTSC) and the Central Valley Regional Water Quality Control Board (CVRWQCB) providing oversight for the RAP and review for the EIR.

The RAP process included public participation measures such as a public notice, a public meeting, and a comment period. The Draft Final RAP was revised based on comments received. On May 18, 2015, DTSC Senior Engineering Geologist Randy S. Adams transmitted revised appendices to Caltrans Project Manager Grace Magsayo, directing that the Draft Final RAP with these appendices be referenced in the Draft EIR and made available for concurrent public review.

## Site Record

The soil stockpiles originated in the early 1960s during the construction of SR-99. Soil excavated from an unlined pond at the former FMC facility, located approximately 500 feet north of the site, was stockpiled within the current Caltrans right-of-way for a planned SR-99/SR-132 interchange. The FMC facility processed barium and strontium minerals from 1929 to approximately 1985, discharging liquid wastes to seven unlined ponds between the early 1950s and late 1970s.

The RAP recommended Alternative #4, containment of approximately 160,000 cubic yards of stockpile soil beneath roadway pavement, behind retaining walls, and behind bridge abutments as part of the SR 132 Project. This was one of four alternatives evaluated in the Feasibility Study:

*   **No action:** Offered the lowest overall protection, lowest regulatory acceptance, was least costly, and most implementable.
*   **Institutional controls:** Included limiting access, inspecting and maintaining fencing, prohibiting soil-disturbing activities, maintaining vegetative cover, and continuing groundwater monitoring. This provided higher protection than no action and had DTSC regulatory acceptance but might be unacceptable to the community.
*   **Removal and offsite disposal:** Provided the greatest overall protection but had the greatest short-term air quality and traffic impacts, the highest cost, and was least implementable due to funding needs and the requirement to replace excavated soil with clean fill for project construction.
*   **Containment through project construction (Alternative #4):** The recommended alternative.

A land use covenant would be recorded to restrict the property's use to non-residential purposes (e.g., precluding residences, schools, daycare centers, and hospitals).

The primary contaminants of potential concern (COPCs) identified in stockpile soil were barium and polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs). Metals and general minerals (e.g., nitrate, total dissolved solids) were identified as primary groundwater COPCs. Cadmium was not considered a COPC in the 2007 Human Health Risk Assessment (HHRA) due to a lack of elevated concentrations in the 2006 site investigation. Strontium was also not considered a COPC because its maximum concentration was more than two orders of magnitude less than the USEPA's residential Regional Screening Level of 47,000 mg/kg.

## Regulatory Status

The RAP was prepared in general accordance with DTSC's *Proven Technologies and Remedies Guidance, Remediation of Metals in Soil* (August 29, 2008). The Statement of Reasons (Appendix C) indicated that the 2007 Risk Assessment and 2013 Risk Assessment Update determined that the stockpiles, as currently managed, do not present an unacceptable risk to human health. Groundwater beneath the stockpiles did not contain COPCs exceeding primary maximum contaminant levels (MCLs) for drinking water. Surveys by the City of Modesto and the Department of Water Resources reported no municipal or domestic use of shallow groundwater within one mile of the stockpiles.

Ecological impacts were addressed in the June 2009 Preliminary Endangerment Assessment. Potential environmental impacts of the recommended remedial alternative were to be evaluated in Caltrans' Draft EIR, with DTSC and CVRWQCB as reviewing agencies. The Final Feasibility Study received DTSC approval on June 30, 2014. The final RAP was subject to DTSC approval for implementation.

## Open Items

At the time of submittal, the Draft Final RAP was pending public comment, and the responsiveness summary had not yet been prepared. Final DTSC approval for implementation was contingent on public review and resolution of comments. The community acceptance criterion, a modifying criterion under the National Contingency Plan (NCP), was deferred as it was not evaluated in the Feasibility Study and was to be addressed after the public comment period. Review and concurrence of the Remedial Design and Implementation Plan and the Operation and Maintenance Plan by DTSC and CVRWQCB were required prior to implementing the recommended remedial action.

### EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The Executive Summary of the Draft Final RAP states it was prepared for the Caltrans Modesto Soil Stockpiles located south of the SR-99/Kansas Avenue interchange in Modesto, Stanislaus County, California. Caltrans was finalizing a draft EIR for the SR-132 West Freeway/Expressway Project in coordination with Stanislaus Council of Governments (StanCOG). The RAP was to supplement the EIR, with DTSC and CVRRWQCB as reviewing agencies for both. The stockpiles originated from an FMC facility and were created in the early 1960s. The RAP summarizes assessments of contaminants and recommends a remedial action.

### Purpose of the RAP

The RAP's purpose is to summarize contaminant characterization, risk assessment, remedial action alternative development, and to make this information available for public review and comment. It includes site physical characteristics, contaminant nature and extent, HHRA results, Applicable or Relevant and Appropriate Requirements (ARARs), a Feasibility Study (FS) summary, conceptual design, land use controls, monitoring plans, implementation schedule, Health and Safety Plan (HSP), CEQA measures, and public participation efforts. The RAP is prepared in general accordance with DTSC's *Proven Technologies and Remedies Guidance, Remediation of Metals in Soil* (August 29, 2008).

### Site Characterization and Contaminants Involved

An Initial Site Assessment (ISA) in 2003 identified stockpiles as potentially containing COPCs from the FMC facility. A Preliminary Site Investigation (PSI) in 2004 found barium concentrations exceeding commercial/industrial California Human Health Screening Levels (CHHSLs) and cadmium exceeding the commercial/industrial CHHSL in Stockpiles #2 and #3. A 2006 site investigation further characterized stockpiles and installed eight groundwater monitoring wells. Initial groundwater samples met drinking water standards (primary and secondary MCLs).

A 2007 HHRA identified barium and PAHs as primary COPCs in soil, and metals and general minerals as primary COPCs in groundwater. Cadmium and strontium were not considered COPCs in the HHRA. The HHRA concluded that soil stockpiles do not pose an unacceptable risk to current or future offsite residents, trespassers, construction workers, or hypothetical future shallow groundwater users. DTSC requested additional information, leading to a Final Preliminary Endangerment Assessment (PEA) in 2009, which concluded that the stockpiles, as managed by Caltrans, do not pose a risk to human health. Caltrans was advised to limit access, maintain the perimeter fence, avoid soil disturbance, maintain vegetative cover, and continue groundwater monitoring.

A second interagency agreement (IA) in 2012 between Caltrans and DTSC outlined tasks for additional characterization, risk evaluation, cleanup level determination, FS, RAP preparation, CEQA compliance, and public participation. Groundwater monitoring was reinitiated from March 2012 to March 2013 bi-monthly, and quarterly thereafter. Two additional wells were installed in May 2012.

### Other Remedial Alternatives Considered

The Feasibility Study (FS) considered "no action," "institutional controls," and "removal and offsite disposal" in addition to containment.

*   **No action:** Lowest overall protection, lowest regulatory acceptance, least costly, most implementable.
*   **Institutional controls:** Required site management activities for protection, had DTSC regulatory acceptance but potential community concerns. Second lowest cost and second most implementable.
*   **Removal and offsite disposal:** Greatest overall protection, greatest short-term impacts (air quality, traffic), highest cost, least implementable due to funding and project construction needs.

The RAP was to be made available for a 30-day public review and comment period, with a public meeting to be held. Comments would be responded to in a responsiveness summary. Revisions would be made based on comments, with potential for re-submission if significant changes were required.

### 1.0 INTRODUCTION

This Draft Final RAP was prepared for the Caltrans Modesto Soil Stockpiles located south of the SR-99/Kansas Avenue interchange in Modesto, Stanislaus County, California. Caltrans was finalizing the draft EIR for the SR-132 West Freeway/Expressway Project (the SR-132 Project) in coordination with StanCOG. DTSC and CVRRWQCB were reviewing agencies for the EIR. The SR-132 Project involves ultimate build-out by 2028, with an interim phase by 2018. The stockpiles, containing elevated barium levels, are planned to be contained within the project as embankment material, retaining wall backfill, and bridge abutments.

### 1.1 Purpose and Organization of the RAP

The purpose of the Draft Final RAP is to describe the remedial action evaluation and selection process, explain the preferred alternative, describe other alternatives considered, and solicit public review and comments. The RAP is organized into sections covering the introduction, nature and extent of impacts, remedial action objectives, summary of the Feasibility Study, preliminary remedial design, land use controls, monitoring and reporting, implementation schedule, health and safety plan, CEQA compliance, and public participation. It is prepared in general accordance with DTSC's *Proven Technologies and Remedies Guidance, Remediation of Metals in Soil*.

### 4.1 Identification and Screening of Technologies

The FS evaluated General Remedial Action (GRA) categories including institutional controls, removal, containment, treatment, and reuse/recycling/reclaim, along with a "no action" alternative. Remedial technologies and process options were screened based on effectiveness, implementability, and cost.

### Modifying Criteria

The modifying criteria considered were regulatory acceptance and community acceptance. Regulatory acceptance assesses technical and administrative issues for DTSC and CVRWQCB. Community acceptance evaluates public issues and concerns and was deferred for evaluation after the public comment period for the Draft Final RAP.

### 11.0 PUBLIC PARTICIPATION

Public participation activities included preparing a baseline community survey (completed by DTSC), preparing a public participation plan (in progress by DTSC), publishing public notices in local newspapers for a minimum of 30 days, distributing fact sheets, conducting a public meeting, and publishing a responsiveness summary. All comments received would be responded to in writing. The 30-day public review period was anticipated for summer 2014. The Draft Final RAP would be revised as necessary, with potential for re-submission if significant changes were required.

### 12.0 LIMITATIONS

This Draft Final RAP was prepared for Caltrans, DTSC, and CVRWQCB. Other parties may rely on it for informational purposes only. It is not a complete set of construction plans or specifications. The recommendations are based on sampling and testing performed to date and are valid as of the RAP's date.

### A.3.4 Alternative 4 – Containment

Containment via capping was assessed as providing the second highest level of protection. It would eliminate exposure routes and minimize stormwater infiltration. Short-term impacts could be minimized with dust controls. Long-term protection would be provided by containment. This alternative was considered compliant with state and federal requirements, anticipated to have regulatory acceptance with DTSC and CVRWQCB oversight, and likely acceptable to the community. It was the third most costly but significantly less than removal, and considered to have good implementability as the stockpiles would be used for their originally intended purpose.
