State Route 132 is the highway alignment whose West Freeway/Expressway Project in Stanislaus County is both the source of the contamination event and its remedy: the soil stockpiles at issue were generated in the early 1960s when [[caltrans]] acquired property from Food Machinery and Chemical Corporation (FMC) to build a segment of State Route 99 north of Kansas Avenue, and decades later the construction of SR 132 south of Kansas Avenue became the mechanism for permanently containing that legacy soil rather than excavating and disposing of it offsite. The project therefore functions simultaneously as the regulatory subject of the remedial action and as the physical remedy itself — the roadway, retaining walls, and bridge abutments of SR 132 are the engineered containment.

## Remedy selected and how it is implemented

The Final Feasibility Study (Geocon, June 2014) and the Draft Final Remedial Action Plan (RAP) recommended Alternative 4: containment of approximately 160,000 cubic yards of stockpile soil beneath roadway pavement, behind retaining walls, and behind bridge abutments of the SR 132 Freeway/Expressway Project between Carpenter Avenue and North Franklin Street, with a clean vegetated soil cap engineered to minimize infiltration where soil is not covered by pavement or structure. DTSC's Statement of Reasons (May 18, 2015) states this alternative was chosen primarily for achievement of remediation goals, implementability, effectiveness, consistency with future land use, and cost effectiveness, and that the cost of this approach — including purchase of clean replacement soil — is approximately 20 times less than excavating and transporting the stockpile material for offsite disposal.

Because the roadway construction is the containment, the project's 100% design plans (Dokken Engineering, submitted 12-10-2018, "Not for Construction") carry remediation-relevant detail directly in the typical cross sections: sheets for Route 132 and the Route 132 Eastbound Couplet specify 12 inches of clean soil within the roadway cross section over the affected stations (e.g., Sta 1135+21.07 to 1141+71.71; Sta 1122+69.67 to 1133+71.07; Sta 1141+71.71 to 1149+43.70), alongside sound walls (1130, 1142), HMA dikes, and retaining structures. These design sheets are the engineering record of how the containment remedy was actually built into the highway.

## Land use restriction and groundwater/surface water protection

Because containment leaves contaminated soil in place under and around the roadway, DTSC's Statement of Reasons ties the remedy's protectiveness to a permanent land use restriction: a land use covenant will be recorded to preclude use of the property for residences, schools, daycare centers, and hospitals, consistent with the Final FS and Draft Final RAP. Protectiveness of groundwater and surface water depends on the integrity of the cover system — pavement, retaining walls, bridge abutments, or the engineered clean vegetated cap — to minimize infiltration and to convey surface water away from the stockpile areas. An Operation and Maintenance Agreement and Plan require maintenance, annual inspections, and reporting for all surfaces overlying the stockpiles, and groundwater and surface water monitoring are required to evaluate whether the covers are in fact preventing infiltration and mobilization of contaminants of potential concern (COPCs); the specific monitoring frequencies and reporting requirements were left to be established in the Remedial Design and Implementation Plan (RDIP), which along with the OMP required review and concurrence by DTSC and the Central Valley Regional Water Quality Control Board before the remedy was implemented.

## Liability allocation

DTSC's preliminary nonbinding allocation of responsibility (NBAR), prepared under Health and Safety Code section 25356.1(e), states that the arbitration provisions that originally gave the NBAR its force (HSC 25356.2–25356.10) were repealed by SB 1018 effective June 27, 2012, leaving promotion of settlement as the NBAR's only remaining function. DTSC concluded that with a small number of potentially responsible parties and with Caltrans, as current property owner, already bearing remediation and O&M costs, a percentage-based allocation would not expedite settlement. The document therefore assigns Caltrans full responsibility for remediation and operation and maintenance costs for the Caltrans Modesto Soil Stockpiles/SR 132 site, including the containment remedy and its associated monitoring programs.

## Field deviations from design during construction

Construction of the SR 132 structures generated soil that did not match the assumptions in the RDIP and had to be handled as an out-of-plan condition. Excavation of Bent 2, an overcrossing structure westerly of SR 99, produced roughly 4,000 cubic yards of native soil, placed as end dumps along roughly 800 feet on the southern portion of the top of Stockpile 2 (partly atop a berm from the Stockpile 2 MSE wall footing excavation previously approved by DTSC as clean cap). Ten samples (STK1–STK10) tested for barium and lead (Geocon Technical Memorandum, October 30, 2020) found barium in all ten samples at 31 to 230 mg/kg, with four of the ten equal to or above the site-specific background threshold of 120 mg/kg, and lead in six of ten samples at 2.6 to 13 mg/kg, within the range of the site-specific background of 3.8 mg/kg. Because barium exceeded the clean cap threshold in four samples, the Bent 2 material did not meet the clean cap criteria of the April 9, 2020 Variance to the RDIP; Geocon proposed, pending DTSC review, that it might still be usable as partial clean cap (6 inches or less) on top of Stockpile 2 given that the planned structural pavement section would overlie it, while clean fill from previously approved sources would still be required on the northerly slope of Stockpile 2. The memo states this information was to be included in the Removal Action Completion Report following Stockpile 1 and 2 clean fill capping — the record does not show DTSC's determination on the partial-cap proposal.
