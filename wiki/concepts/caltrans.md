Caltrans is the California Department of Transportation, the property owner, project proponent, and the entity legally responsible for both the SR 132 West Freeway/Expressway Project and the three Modesto Soil Stockpiles that sit within its own state right-of-way south of Kansas Avenue. Caltrans occupies a dual role in this case file: it is the CEQA/NEPA lead agency for the highway project and, simultaneously, the responsible party that acquired contaminated soil in the early 1960s, stockpiled it, managed it for five decades under [[dtsc]] and [[central-valley-rwqcb]] oversight, and ultimately proposed to permanently contain it within the highway structure itself. The remedy Caltrans selected — encapsulating the stockpiles behind retaining walls, bridge abutments, and pavement — makes the freeway project and the environmental cleanup structurally and legally inseparable: approval of one depended on approval of the other.

## Origin of Caltrans's liability

The stockpiles originated when Caltrans purchased a 4.3-acre parcel at the southwest corner of the Food Machinery and Chemical Corporation (FMC) Modesto Processing Plant to construct the State Route 99 Modesto bypass. FMC and its predecessors had processed barium and strontium ores (barite and celestite) and discharged residual liquids to unlined ponds on that parcel. During bypass construction, Caltrans excavated soil from and around those ponds and relocated it to its present three-stockpile configuration (Stockpile 1, ~2.5 acres; Stockpile 2, ~7.6 acres; Stockpile 3, ~2.5 acres), totaling roughly 160,000 cubic yards. Caltrans has managed this material on its own right-of-way since the 1960s.

## Management pending remedy selection

While the remedy was being developed, Caltrans's obligations were interim and administrative: maintaining perimeter fencing, restricting site access to authorized workers, mowing vegetative cover annually (at minimum before July 4th, per the fire-hazard commitment described in Response PH4-1), and conducting groundwater and surface water/stormwater monitoring under DTSC and RWQCB oversight. In its formal response to public comments (I2-2), Caltrans stated it had not received a violation or enforcement action related to groundwater or surface water monitoring at the site and that, to its knowledge, it had complied with all site monitoring requirements — a position DTSC did not contradict in the same document. Caltrans also represented, in response to a comment alleging a 2014 brush fire caused by encroachment, that only one fire had been reported at the stockpiles.

Public commenters (notably Bruce Frohman, Rhett Calkins, and Julie Brughelli at the February 22, 2017 hearing) alleged specific management failures — unrestricted public access, stormwater runoff leaving the site, unmowed vegetation, and undocumented drainage infrastructure connecting Stockpiles 1/2 to Stockpile 3's basin near SR 99. These allegations were entered into the administrative record as part of Appendix J but were not independently substantiated in the responses; Caltrans and DTSC's replies restated existing management practices rather than confirming or denying the specific incidents.

## Selection of containment as the remedy

Caltrans, under DTSC and RWQCB oversight, developed the Draft Final Remedial Action Plan (RAP) evaluating multiple alternatives, including Alternative 3 (Removal — excavation and offsite disposal of the full 160,000 cubic yards) and Alternative 4 (Containment). Caltrans and DTSC selected Alternative 4 as the recommended alternative: stockpiles retained in place behind retaining walls and bridge abutments, beneath roadway pavement, with clean fill cover on unpaved portions. The stated rationale, repeated verbatim across dozens of comment responses, was that Removal was technically feasible and ARAR-compliant but caused the greatest short-term air-quality impacts during excavation and was less cost-effective than Containment.

Selecting Containment tied the fate of the stockpiles directly to the SR 132 West freeway design: Stockpiles 1 and 2 remain in place but require height increases with clean soil to meet the elevated roadway's design grade, and the majority of Stockpile 3 is to be consolidated within the SR 132 Overcrossing abutment at Needham Avenue, with excess soil from that consolidation placed atop Stockpile 2 and covered with clean soil.

## Ongoing and future obligations under the selected remedy

Adoption of Alternative 4 imposes continuing obligations on Caltrans beyond construction:

- Caltrans must enter into an Operation and Maintenance Agreement with DTSC and prepare an O&M Plan for DTSC review and approval.
- The O&M Plan requires an annual inspection of the pavement and other containment features.
- Groundwater monitoring continues under the remedy.
- DTSC evaluates the containment remedy every five years to confirm it is performing as designed.
- During construction, Caltrans is required to wet down stockpile soils in work areas before and during work, and to conduct air monitoring in those areas, to control dust.

## Procedural role as CEQA/NEPA lead agency

Caltrans served as lead agency for the joint Draft EIR/EA and Draft Final RAP public circulation (January 18–March 17, 2017), including a joint public hearing with DTSC, RWQCB, and StanCOG on February 22, 2017 at Mark Twain Junior High School. Of 305 total comments received, 62 addressed the Draft Final RAP specifically; DTSC, in consultation with RWQCB, drafted responses to stockpile-related comments, which Caltrans formally adopted as its own in Appendix J of the Final EIR/EA (response letter dated March 13, 2018, from DTSC Project Manager Randy Adams). This arrangement — DTSC answering substantively, Caltrans concurring by reference — appears consistently through the comment-response record and reflects the underlying allocation of responsibility: DTSC as remedy regulator, Caltrans as landowner and implementer bound by DTSC's determinations.

## Litigation-relevant representations

Because the RAP response letter is part of the administrative record, several Caltrans/DTSC joint representations are load-bearing for any future liability review:

- The stockpiles "do not pose an unacceptable risk to human health" for Caltrans workers, trespassers, or adjacent residents, based on current management activities (repeated verbatim across at least a dozen individual responses).
- Maximum surface soil concentrations of arsenic, carcinogenic PAHs, and vanadium fall within local background ranges; barium and nickel maxima are below U.S. EPA safe-use concentrations; [[strontium]] and nitrate are below any level considered unsafe.
- No cancer-causing chemicals have been detected in groundwater; low arsenic concentrations detected are attributed to natural background rather than the stockpiles.
- Vibration from highway traffic will not cause contaminants to migrate into groundwater (response to comment I7-1/PH17 series).
- Regarding the unrelated 2012 Modesto Ramp Rehabilitation Project at SR 99/Kansas Avenue, excavated soil tested below screening thresholds but was nonetheless sent to a Class II landfill (Forward Inc., Manteca) rather than a standard Class III facility, because sampling indicated the soil could qualify as designated waste — a decision Caltrans staff, per a resident's account, could not clearly explain when questioned at a DTSC meeting.
